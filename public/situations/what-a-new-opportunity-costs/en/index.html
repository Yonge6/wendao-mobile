A Pay Rise. Three Time Zones Included.

Read the comic reflection

The opening turns the pleasure of a raise into something almost physically oversized. The woman in the emerald suit holds a JOB OFFER marked ANNUAL SALARY +50%, while an enormous golden +50% soon fills the next panel behind her. Her imagined rewards float around it: a city apartment, travel, a handbag, a restaurant meal and a brighter future. The Art Deco rays, dark green interiors and gold geometry make the opportunity look like a stage premiere. That exaggeration is also the first joke: the salary has already become enormous before the rest of her life has had time to enter the picture.

The cream-suited man does not puncture her excitement; he simply asks, "What are the conditions?" The visual answer arrives as three compact slices of ordinary life. Her commute stretches from one hour to two, overseas work produces frequent night meetings, and family time becomes harder to protect. Three clocks above the nighttime desk turn the title's "three time zones" into a literal prop, while the conversation with her mother gives the cost a human scale. The woman is not suddenly told the opportunity is bad. She is simply shown that the extra money is not the only thing being exchanged.

The turning point is practical rather than dramatic. In panel 07, the woman sits with a notebook and a board divided into BENEFITS and COSTS, listing higher pay, a bigger platform, challenge and growth beside commute x2, night calls, less family time, energy and schedule changes. Then she and the man rehearse the conversation instead of rehearsing a refusal. Her request sheet names remote days, a travel allowance, limits on night calls, family time and team working arrangements. The line "No automatic refusal, no automatic yes" captures what the pictures are doing: replacing a vague reaction with negotiable terms and visible boundaries.

The video-call panel makes the trade-off specific. The recruiter on the screen confirms two remote days each week and a commuting allowance, while two weekly night meetings remain fixed for the overseas team. The checklist visibly marks the first two items while leaving the night-meeting condition separate. The next panel then places those abstract terms into a real week: Tuesday and Thursday carry 20:00-22:00 Night Call blocks, Wednesday reserves 18:30-20:00 for Family Dinner, Friday gets coffee, and the weekend becomes two simple trees marked Rest. The joke is gentle but useful: an offer can sound manageable in a sentence and look very different once it occupies colored squares on a calendar.

By panel 11, the woman has decided to accept. She does not call the offer perfect; she says it is "Worth choosing at this stage," while the man answers, "A decision that fits you." The final panel softens the office calculation with coffee, cake, flowers and both characters smiling. Her closing thought, "Salary is addition. Life needs the whole balance sheet," is followed by the man's reminder that living well matters in the long run, while the footer lands on "A salary can sparkle. A calendar tells the truth." As a contemporary reflection, this can sit beside Chapter 44's attention to contentment and knowing when to stop: not as the only meaning of the classical passage, but as one everyday way of noticing what is enough, what has a cost, and what one is genuinely willing to carry.

Therefore, knowing contentment avoids disgrace; knowing when to stop avoids danger; thus one can endure.

A little room for today

When an opportunity looks especially attractive, try making your own BENEFITS and COSTS page, then place the important conditions into an actual week. Mark what could be negotiated, what would remain fixed and what time you want to protect. The exercise is not meant to produce an automatic yes or no; it simply lets the full shape of the choice become visible.
