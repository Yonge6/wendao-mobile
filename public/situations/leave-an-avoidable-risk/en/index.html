Saving Three Minutes. Risking the Whole Day.

Read the comic reflection

The opening joke comes from how quickly the boy turns urgency into permission. He checks his watch, worries that three more minutes will make him late, and the conversation immediately turns to a shortcut. By the next panel, the red-and-white barrier, the large "PATH CLOSED / DETOUR" sign, and the separate "MAINTENANCE / THIS WAY" board make the situation visually unambiguous. The girl points out that the path is closed, but he still answers, "Just a few steps!" The comedy is not that he cannot read the warning; it is that he is already negotiating with it.

That self-justification gets even funnier in panels three and four. When the girl says the detour adds only a few minutes, the boy stands proudly and replies, "I'm not scared. I'm efficient." His imagination then inflates an ordinary commute into a tiny heroic epic: he pictures himself climbing dramatically with a red "FEARLESS" flag while the thought "Real experts never detour" floats above him and "SHORTCUTS ARE COURAGE" appears inside the fantasy. The thought bubble matters because the comic clearly presents this as his private self-image, not as advice from the story.

Reality interrupts the imaginary hero at exactly the right moment. The girl shouts "Wait!" as the boy's shoe skids beside a puddle, with a bright yellow "SLIPPERY" warning sign standing almost comically close by. His "Whoa!" replaces the heroic slogans in an instant. The turning point is deliberately small: in panel six he does not win an argument or perform a dramatic rescue; he simply says, "Let me pull my foot back first." The girl answers, "That's brave enough," while the phone already allows for the practical possibility that they may be a few minutes late.

The lower page makes the detour feel ordinary rather than punitive. The girl points to a sign reading "VIEWPOINT / OPEN PATH / ABOUT 8 MIN," with the closed path clearly marked "NO ENTRY," and says, "This way. A few minutes longer, but open." Soon the boy can joke that the extra walking is a little exercise, and the girl replies, "Arriving safely counts!" They even stop for the visual punch line of a roadside cat: he exclaims "A cat!", the cat contributes a perfectly timed "MEOW," and she reminds him, smiling and holding her phone, "Not too long. We're still on a schedule!"

The ending quietly shrinks the original three-minute crisis back to its proper size. They catch Bus 318, the boy says the trip went smoothly even though they are a little late, and the girl sends a straightforward message: "On our way safely. A few minutes late. Thanks for understanding!" His relieved "Much better" shows that the delay has become something manageable instead of something worth gambling over. In the final panel, warm sunset light fills the bus window as he thinks, "Today's bravest move was stepping back." The closing line, "Some detours come with a better view," links that modest reversal to the supplied Dao De Jing excerpt as a contemporary reflection: not as the one fixed meaning of the ancient text, but as a story about declining an avoidable danger instead of manufacturing one.

Because there is no place of death there.

A little room for today

The next time haste, inconvenience, or pride makes a risky shortcut feel strangely reasonable, look first at what the situation is actually telling you: what is closed, what is open, and how much time the safer route really adds. If the difference is only a few minutes, try pulling your foot back before deciding what comes next.
