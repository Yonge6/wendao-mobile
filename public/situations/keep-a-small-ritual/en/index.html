My Self-Discipline Fits in a Flowerpot

Read the comic reflection

The opening joke is built into the contrast between the young woman's confidence and the room's immediate reply. In panel 01, she pins up a beautifully decorated "MY NEW PLAN" with RUN, READ, and PIANO waiting in neat rows while declaring, "Starting tomorrow, I'll be a whole new person." Panel 02 answers with a phone at her ear, a laptop or folder wedged against her body, shopping bags, parcels, loose papers, and the black-and-white cat watching the pile-up from below. Her line, "The new me isn't here yet. The old workload is," turns the ambitious makeover into a visual collision between an ideal schedule and an already crowded life.

By "Day Three," the comedy has gone quiet. The young woman sits curled on the floor in patterned socks, surrounded by crumpled paper, an open notebook, and a mug, while the same plan still hangs behind her and the red heart pot by the window is visibly wilted. "Failed again" makes several unchecked ambitions feel like one sweeping verdict. The turning point arrives when the gray-haired neighbour appears in her blue floral apron holding the blue watering can. She asks only, "Watered your plant today?" The young woman answers, "I can't even manage my life," but the neighbour does not add another life-management system to the wall.

Panel 05 lowers the scale of the problem with a wonderfully literal prop: the neighbour simply passes over the watering can and says, "The plant didn't ask you to sort out your whole life first." In panel 06, the young woman waters the still-drooping plant and asks, "That's it?" while the neighbour, chin resting comfortably on her hand, replies, "That's it for today." The black-and-white cat remains nearby as a quiet witness. Nothing has suddenly transformed: the leaves are not magically lush, the checklist is not completed, and there is no triumphant montage. The joke is that the supposedly enormous project of becoming disciplined has, for the moment, become one very ordinary minute with a flowerpot.

The second page keeps that smallness without turning it into another rigid rule. Before leaving with her backpack, the young woman checks the plant and says, "Not thirsty today? I'll just say hello," so care means observing its condition rather than automatically watering it. Later, beside a laptop, stacks of work, a desk lamp, and the attentive cat, she wipes a leaf and notices that "A small thing can actually wait for me." The calendar in "Sometimes I Forget" makes the next joke explicit: red marks fill several days, one square is left open, and her response is "Missed yesterday. No detention today." Continuity here includes gaps instead of treating every missed day as a disciplinary hearing.

The ending shows change through details rather than a victory speech. The young woman peers through a magnifying glass at a fresh leaf while the neighbour says, "Growth isn't a daily performance." In "A Smaller Plan," the familiar RUN, READ, and PIANO list remains, but "Two pages today still counts" replaces the earlier demand for a total reinvention. Finally, at sunset, she reads beside the healthier plant while the black-and-white cat sleeps on the floral cushion, saying, "My self-discipline isn't impressive. But it's alive." As a contemporary association with Chapter 6, this fits the image of something continuing quietly without being forced into spectacle: the comic does not claim to translate the ancient line literally, but it imagines continuity as an action small enough to remain available tomorrow.

It goes on, as though barely present; use it, and it is never exhausted.

English rendering of the Silk B base reading excerpt
A little room for today

Choose one small action that can fit inside the day you already have, perhaps two pages, one checked leaf, or one minute of tidying. Let it become smaller when the day is crowded, and if you miss it once, simply notice where you are and continue when it makes sense. After a few days, check whether the action still fits rather than turning it into another test.
