Most of My Hard Work Is Proving I Work Hard

Wendao editorial · AI-assisted editing

Save complete comic ↓

Read the comic reflection

The opening joke is not that Alex is avoiding work. He looks almost excessively prepared for it. In his navy suit, he photographs a desk stacked with books labeled PROJECT, RESEARCH, DESIGN, and PROGRESS, while a board announces PLAN, FOCUS, EXECUTE, and FINISH. Even his mug says "GOOD WORK BETTER ME." The scene has every visual signal of serious productivity, yet the phone is already becoming the main character: the carefully arranged evidence of working is taking up space that could have gone to the work itself.

That joke grows through the next updates. Alex photographs the setup again after five minutes, then his feed neatly records "STARTING!," "ORGANIZING!," and "THINKING!" at 09:05, 09:10, and 09:15. When his coworker asks how the project is going, he answers by holding up the phone and pointing to his constant progress. The turning point comes at report time. The board beside him still has RESULTS, DATA, and NEXT STEPS unchecked, and in the next panel his coworker asks the painfully practical question: is there anything they can actually put in the report? Alex looks down at the phone and admits there probably is not yet.

The lower page changes the behavior without turning Alex into a miraculous productivity machine. He switches off FIVE-MINUTE UPDATES, DAILY FRAGMENTS, and RANDOM PHOTOS, while leaving ESSENTIAL SYNC on and keeping MILESTONES and IMPORTANT ISSUES checked. The black mug beside him now reads "LESS BROADCAST MORE WORK," turning the earlier mug joke into a quieter reminder. With fewer status rituals competing for attention, he works on Module A. The visible checklist covers DATA, IMPLEMENTATION, INITIAL TESTS, and OUTPUT, while the progress bar remains at 80%, making it clear that this is meaningful progress rather than a claim that the whole project is finished.

The comic also keeps the process imperfect. Alex tells his coworker that he has a working first version and will organize the data before syncing with her. Later he admits that he hit a snag midway and spent time solving it. The important change is not silence: he still communicates, and she still offers help and review. What disappears is the need to broadcast every intermediate motion as proof of effort. Their conversation is now attached to something concrete—a working version, a solved problem, and a reviewable result—rather than a stream of photos showing that activity is happening.

The ending makes the contrast visible on the laptop. Instead of a gallery of status updates, the project stage report is one page, led by "Core first version completed," with GOAL ACHIEVED, CORE FUNCTION DEMO, DATA RESULTS, and NEXT STEPS, plus attachments such as DATA DETAILS.xlsx and TEST RECORDS.pdf. In the final panel Alex leans back beneath a note reading "LESS BROADCAST MORE WORK," while the page closes with "Looking busy fades. What you make is what stays." Read beside Chapter 38, this can be a contemporary reflection on preferring substance to display: not an instruction to stop communicating, but an invitation to let communication gather around work that has actually become usable.

Therefore the great person dwells in what is substantial, not in what is thin; dwells in what is real, not in what is showy.

A little room for today

Choose one thing you are already working on and skip one unnecessary progress display today. Use that small pocket of time to finish a piece someone can actually open, read, test, or use. When you do need to sync, share that concrete result instead of documenting every step that led to it.
