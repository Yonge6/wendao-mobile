One Step Left. Time for a Thesis on Shoelaces.

Read the comic reflection

The opening joke is taken over by an enormous sneaker. The red SEND button is already waiting on the monitor, yet the man in the yellow shirt is bent over the shoe as if its laces deserve urgent research. His friend in the green jacket immediately spots the detour: "Why research shoelaces first?" From there, the same hesitation keeps changing costumes. The filename multiplies into FINAL, FINAL 2, and REALLY FINAL; the submission note grows more ceremonious; and, in the most exaggerated image, he literally polishes a giant red SEND key while insisting that "The final step needs ceremony." The work is close to done, but his attention has begun orbiting the finish line instead of crossing it.

His friend does not simply tell him to hurry. In panel 05 she asks the more useful question: "What's actually missing for delivery?" That question changes the structure of the problem. The first page ends with an ESSENTIAL CHECKS board, and the second page makes the distinction even sharper: ESSENTIAL contains Opens, Correct version, All pages, and Limitations, while LATER contains Filename, Note wording, and Thumbnail. The joke about excessive polishing remains, but the checklist gives the anxiety somewhere practical to go. What might cause a real failure is checked now; what could merely be nicer is allowed to wait.

The checking then proves its value. In panel 08, the man nearly chooses OLD before identifying SUBMIT THIS, while FINAL 2 sits beside it like one more branch of the file family tree. His friend's line, "Good. The file family tree ends with you," turns a familiar version-control mess into a small punchline. Then another concrete problem appears: page 4 has been hidden underneath the others. With pages 1 through 6 spread across the desk beside the keyboard and blue mug, the delivery check becomes a miniature detective scene. Her response—"At least it was hiding, not running away"—keeps the moment light while showing that vague unease has finally been converted into specific things they can verify.

The next turn is not toward perfection but toward disclosure. On the laptop, the man writes KNOWN LIMITATIONS and lists "Some diagrams still drafts" and "Mobile preview may be slow." His friend answers, "Clarity beats pretending it's perfect." That visible shift matters: instead of trying to erase every imperfection before the deadline, he makes the remaining imperfections explicit. Once the essential checks are complete, she says, "Checked? Send." He presses the giant button, the small ON TIME! sign appears beside the clock, and his answer—"Pressing it. No more polishing."—finally turns preparation into delivery.

The ending reconnects the whole sequence to the opening joke. The screen now reads SENT, the man's body is relaxed, and the oversized sneaker returns prominently in the foreground. He does not claim perfect satisfaction; he says, "Not zero regrets. Ready to take responsibility." His friend closes the loop with, "Defend the shoelace thesis another day." The comic therefore echoes the supplied line from the Daodejing without treating itself as a literal translation: it focuses on that vulnerable moment when something is nearly complete and extra refinements, repeated checking, or last-minute additions can interfere with finishing. Its practical distinction is modest rather than absolute—separate errors that could break the delivery from improvements that can safely continue afterward.

In undertaking affairs, people constantly fail when they are on the verge of completion.

A little room for today

If you are stuck at the final step, make two columns like the comic does. In the first, write what could actually cause the delivery to fail if you do not verify it now. In the second, write what could still be improved later. Check the first column item by item, state any known limitations clearly, and then decide whether the work is ready to be sent responsibly.
