I Cared This Soup to Death

Read the comic reflection

The joke begins with the cook's remarkably busy hands. In the first six panels, the woman in the red floral blouse barely gives the black soup pot a moment alone: she lifts the lid, adds vegetables, sprinkles in more salt, lowers the heat, and even moves the pot for more even cooking. The pale-shirted man starts out cheerfully waiting for dinner, but every improvement creates another delay. His patience turns into the page's best running joke when he observes, "This soup's in a meeting about its next steps." The soup has stopped feeling like dinner and started feeling like a project under permanent review.

The turning point arrives in panel 06. The cook lifts the lid yet again and wonders, "Maybe add something?" Instead of proposing one more ingredient or technique, the man answers, "What it needs now may be... your absence." The second page shows what that actually means. She does not abandon the pot or stop caring about it; she checks that there are enough vegetables and that the heat is safe, then chooses a steady simmer. The distinction is practical: necessary conditions are confirmed, but continuous intervention is no longer treated as necessary.

That new restraint is immediately tested. In panel 09, the cook reaches toward the steaming pot and admits, "Just one more peek..." The man redirects her attention with the wonderfully modest suggestion, "Look at the tea instead." A cup of tea beside the stove becomes a tiny alternative to another unnecessary inspection. The humor works because the urge has not magically disappeared; she still wants to interfere, but now there is a small pause between the impulse and the action.

The comic then carries the same pattern from the kitchen to the worktable. Beside a stack of pages, a note reading "WORK FOR 20 MINUTES FIRST," and a timer showing 20:00, the cook recognizes that she has been treating her draft like the soup: "Stop tearing it up while writing." The man's reply, "Give it an uninterrupted stretch," turns the cooking lesson into a concrete working method. By panel 11, the paper is labeled "PROJECT DRAFT — FIRST VERSION," and their exchange — "Less interference. More progress" and "Finally building without dismantling" — makes the parallel visible rather than merely explaining it.

The final panel brings both threads to the table. The cook and the man are smiling over bowls of finished soup, while the document beside them has advanced to "PROJECT DRAFT — COMPLETED VERSION." Her line, "Today's soup survived my care," keeps the self-mockery gentle, and his answer, "Both it and the draft can breathe again," lets the pot and the manuscript share the punch line. In relation to the Daodejing line, the comic offers a contemporary reflection rather than a claim about its single definitive meaning: ingredients, heat, safety, and real problems still deserve attention, but once the essentials are in place, constant handling can become another obstacle. Sometimes doing less simply gives an ongoing process enough room to finish.

Governing a great state is like cooking a small fish.

A little room for today

Choose one thing today that you have been repeatedly checking, revising, or restarting. Confirm the essentials first, then give it twenty uninterrupted minutes. If you feel the urge to lift the metaphorical lid again, take a sip of water or tea and wait until the twenty minutes are over before deciding whether anything actually needs changing.
