My Life Is Just One Juicer Away

Save complete comic ↓

Read the comic reflection

The joke begins in panel 01 with an extremely ordinary promise: the brown-haired woman lies under a pink blanket beside the sleeping calico cat and tells herself, "One quick look. Then sleep." Her phone is already showing glossy green-drink imagery, and the next panel immediately enlarges that tiny scroll into a fantasy morning of bright windows, a city skyline, a yoga mat, sunshine, and a green juice in hand. The contrast is the first visual punch line: she is still in bed, but the advertisement has already furnished an entire future for her.

Panels 03 through 06 make that fantasy increasingly physical. Sitting in a cluttered room with a notebook, she decides, "No wonder I lack discipline. I don't own a juicer." At 00:23 she taps "ORDER NOW" while declaring, "Buy a machine. Get a whole new life delivered." Three days later, her striped-shirt friend arrives to find boxes labeled "JUICER," "YOGA MAT," and "JOURNAL," turning self-improvement into a literal warehouse joke. Then the bright promise meets the kitchen counter: the woman, now in pink pajamas, sits beside a juicer, dirty parts, fruit scraps, peels, bowls, and dishes, sighing that two minutes of drinking have produced thirty minutes of washing.

The second page does not declare the juicer a mistake. Instead, panel 07 shows the next recommendation arriving before the first purchase has even settled into daily life: an "AUTOMATIC JUICER CLEANER." The brown-haired woman wonders, "Maybe I still need one more thing..." while her friend looks on. The turning point comes in panel 08, where the striped-shirt friend simply holds out an orange and asks, "Do you want juice, or do you want that morning?" The nearby "GOOD THINGS TAKE TIME" poster quietly reinforces the pause without turning the conversation into a lecture.

Panels 09 through 11 shift attention from upgrading the setup to testing the desire. In warm daylight, with the calico cat nearby, the woman peels and eats an actual orange and jokes, "Turns out sunshine has no minimum order." She then writes "CHECK TOMORROW" in a notebook, listing the automatic cleaner and a sports bottle, while her friend suggests letting the impulse "work a night shift" in the cart. The next day, the cleaner is crossed out but the sports bottle remains checked. That detail matters: the comic does not treat every purchase as foolish. One accessory loses its urgency overnight, while something she still finds useful stays on the list.

The ending returns to the morning she originally wanted, but without pretending that life has been perfected. In panel 12 the two women sit together laughing over oranges, a plate of orange wedges, mugs, and the still-visible juicer at the side of the table. The brown-haired woman says, "Today I'll just live. No upgrade required." The closing line, "Spend less time on what you're missing. Notice the life you already have," connects the story loosely to Chapter 3's concern with desire and external stimulation: not as a literal translation of the ancient text, and not as a rule against buying things, but as an invitation to notice how easily a displayed object can arrive bundled with an imagined identity or lifestyle.

Do not put desirable things on display, so that the people are not thrown into disorder.

A little room for today

Put one thing you suddenly want into a "check tomorrow" list. The next day, write down the specific problem it would solve and the effort of using, cleaning, storing, or maintaining it. If a simple option is already available, try that once first; if the purchase still makes sense afterward, keep it.
