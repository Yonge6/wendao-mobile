Making Everyone Punctual, Starting with My Eight-Minute Delay

Wendao editorial · AI-assisted editing

Save complete comic ↓

Read the comic reflection

The opening joke is visual before it becomes verbal. The bearded man in the red plaid shirt sits at home holding his phone and declaring, "Punctuality matters! Everyone should be on time." Beside him are books labeled "Plan," "Discipline," and "A Better Me," an orange cat is sleeping peacefully, and the wall insists, "PUNCTUALITY STARTS WITH ME." In the very next panel, however, he is still at the doorway pulling on a shoe while saying "Almost there!" and a large notice reads "ALREADY LATE." His principle has arrived well before he has.

At the café, the long-haired woman in the blue scarf does not argue abstractly about whether he is a punctual person. She teases him with the much more specific line, "Your 'almost there' seems to arrive punctually every day." Then she produces two plain pieces of evidence: one records "ALMOST THERE!" at 08:12, while the other says "STILL HOME" and "3.8 KM TO GO." The next panel sharpens the joke when she says, "Your voice note arrived first. You came later." His reply, "I thought it was close enough...," turns the scene from accusation into slightly sheepish recognition.

The turning point happens when the problem becomes measurable. At the table, the man stops treating every journey as one vague "almost there" and studies an "Estimated Travel Time" sheet listing 25 minutes by car, 40 by bus, and 60 on foot. On the next page he leaves with a concrete message: "Leaving now. Route estimate says 7:40." The route card breaks that estimate into an eight-minute walk, 28 minutes of transit, and another four-minute walk, with an arrival of 07:40. When traffic changes the plan, he updates it again: "There's a delay ahead. I'll be six minutes late. Please order first."

That clearer information changes what everyone else can do. The woman replies "OK!" and, instead of waiting indefinitely, tells the server, "We'll order now. No need to wait." The server answers, "Knowing the time helps." When the man reaches the café, the joke is no longer that he pretended to be nearby; it is that he can proudly say, "I said six minutes late. And I was six minutes late." Her response, "That's much more useful than 'almost there,'" captures the practical improvement without turning him into a suddenly perfect person.

The last two panels show the change becoming ordinary rather than heroic. Over a later meal, the woman says, "You've been reliable lately," another friend adds, "Easier for everyone to plan," and the man concludes, "Keeping a promise beats announcing a principle." In the final panel he is already seated at the table with a mug, flowers, and reading material while the woman approaches from the doorway, and he jokes, "World order's unfinished. At least this table isn't waiting for me." The closing banner, "Replace 'almost there' with a real time. Dinner stays warmer," brings the grand opening rhetoric back down to one small, observable habit. Read alongside the supplied Daodejing line, this can be understood as a contemporary reflection on beginning with one's own conduct rather than as a claim that the comic is a literal or exclusive interpretation of the ancient text.

Cultivate it in oneself, and its virtue becomes genuine.

A little room for today

Next time you are meeting someone, try replacing "almost there" with a specific arrival time. Check the real route first, send your estimate, and if the situation changes, send an updated time. Then simply notice whether that small bit of precision makes the wait easier for both people to arrange.
