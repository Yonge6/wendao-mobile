The opening joke comes from watching a very small request become enormous in the woman's mind. In panel 01, the desk calendar says only "FRI 14:00 / IMPORTANT PLANS," yet she is already wondering, "Is my reason important enough?" By panel 02, she is typing "LEAVE REQUEST · DRAFT 1" while asking whether she first has to prove that she deserves a break. The office is calm, but her document is already stretching beyond the scale of the actual problem: she needs half a day off, yet she has begun treating the request like an examination of her worthiness.

Her colleague arrives just as the explanation starts expanding into a miniature archive. The long sheets in panel 03 are headed "PERSONAL SITUATION," "PAST CONTRIBUTIONS," and "CURRENT DIFFICULTIES," while she worries that a short request may not be convincing. He asks, "Leave request or defense of your entire life?" The joke becomes even sharper in panel 04, where the wall displays "OVERTIME," "HELPING," and "ALWAYS ON TIME." When she asks whether she should explain how hard she usually works, he answers, "You're asking for half a day, not Employee of the Year." His humor does not dismiss her concern; it exposes how far the request has drifted from what the process actually needs.

The turning point begins when her colleague changes the question. Instead of asking how impressive or persuasive the explanation sounds, he asks, "What does the process actually need?" The board in panels 06 and 07 reduces the answer to three items: "TIME," "HANDOVER," and "NECESSARY REASON." The woman immediately sees the difference and asks, "Not my entire life story?" She then returns to the computer and says, "I'll rewrite it to fit the process." His reply, "Clear beats tragic," captures the comic's central shift: the goal is no longer to build an emotional case, but to provide the information another person needs in order to respond.

The second page makes that change visible. When the woman considers adding a sheet that begins "ALL THESE YEARS, I'VE WORKED SO HARD...", her colleague jokes, "Save that for the autobiography." She instead sends a short request stating, "Friday, 14:00–18:00. Requesting half a day for an important personal commitment. Work handed over." In the comic, the laptop soon displays "LEAVE REQUEST: APPROVED," and she is surprised enough to ask, "Approved already?" He answers that clear information saves everyone effort. That quick approval is the outcome shown in this particular story; real leave requests can still depend on workplace rules, staffing, documentation requirements, and other circumstances.

The ending quietly connects the office comedy with the supplied passage from Chapter 72. In the final panel, the woman places the huge discarded draft into a drawer and says, "Looking after myself doesn't need to start with winning a debate." The closing line underneath makes the joke concrete: "A leave request needs clear details, not an autobiography." Read alongside the chapter excerpt, the scene suggests a restrained form of self-knowledge and self-regard: she can recognize her own need without turning it into a public performance of merit, and she can respect a shared process without surrendering every private detail. The comic offers this as a contemporary life reflection, not as the only possible interpretation of the classical text.

A little room for today

The next time you need to request leave or make a personal scheduling request, first check the actual process requirements. Write down the time, the work handover, and whatever explanation is genuinely necessary. Then look at anything else you were tempted to include and ask: does this help the other person make the arrangement, or am I adding it mainly to prove that I deserve to ask? Keep those two kinds of information separate, while still providing any additional material the real policy requires.
