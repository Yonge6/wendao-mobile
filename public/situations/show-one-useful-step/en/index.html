A Hundred Explanations. Or One Cup Together.

Wendao editorial · AI-assisted editing

Save complete comic ↓

Read the comic reflection

The opening joke is that the more carefully the man explains, the harder coffee seems to make. Standing beside the silver espresso machine, he points through a whole technical vocabulary: grind, distribute, tamp, extract, about 92 degrees Celsius, and nine bars. The woman in the brown sweater rests her head on one hand and admits, "So many terms. I can't remember them all." Behind them, botanical coffee drawings and the little "Anatomy of Coffee" display make the room look increasingly like a classroom, while the empty cup quietly reminds us that nobody has actually made coffee yet.

The explanation keeps expanding. A wall diagram compares "TOO COARSE," "ABOUT RIGHT," and "TOO FINE," then the man demonstrates tamping with "15–20 kilos of force" and describes a shot lasting "25–30 seconds" for 36 grams. Each answer produces another anxious question: "Will I break it?" and "Will I press the wrong button?" By panel 05, even the visual language has become heavy: a stack of books labeled Grind, Distribute, Tamp, Attach Handle, Extract, and Temperature / Pressure / Time sits beside them. The turning point arrives when the man finally notices the obvious problem and says, "Talking alone is a lot. What if we try this?"

The second page changes the teaching method rather than adding another explanation. The man says, "I'll do one. Watch along," and the long lecture collapses into a simple visual sequence: GRIND, TAMP, EXTRACT. Then panel 08 makes the new rule explicit: "Remember the actions first, not the vocabulary." Four close-up boxes show GRIND, LEVEL, TAMP, and LOCK IN. The woman answers, "I can follow this!" The important shift is visible in their bodies as well as their words: instead of sitting beside a growing pile of information, they are now leaning over the machine and doing something together.

Once the woman takes over, mistakes become small, local, and fixable. In panel 09 she presses the tamper herself, with loose coffee grounds scattered across the counter, and asks, "Like this?" The man does not restart the entire lesson; he simply points to the bed and says, "A little more level here. That's it." In the next panel he points out where the cup should go, and she jokes, "Oh! Nearly gave the coffee a free-fall experiment!" That warm joke matters because the error is not turned into evidence that she cannot do it. It is just the next thing to adjust.

The payoff is deliberately ordinary. In panel 11, espresso finally streams from the machine into the large white cup, and the woman leans in with delighted surprise: "It's coming out! Actual coffee!" The man answers, "No academic conference required." By the final panel, both have moved away from the machinery and are holding cups at the table. He says that knowledge has moved "from my mouth to my hands," and she finishes the thought with "And into our cups!" The closing line, "Fewer explanations. One cup made together," offers a contemporary echo of Chapter 43: not a literal translation of the ancient text, but a small domestic example of how less verbal control and more shared action can sometimes make understanding possible.

Teaching without words, the benefit of non-action—few under heaven can attain this.

A little room for today

If you notice yourself repeating the same explanation today, try turning one part of it into a shared demonstration. Do it once together, then let the other person take the next step. Add a short correction only when a real difficulty appears, and see whether the task becomes clearer through action.
